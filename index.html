Mapping shallow waters from space

Led by Dr. Anders Knudby, we use satellite, airborne and in situ observations to map and monitor shallow coastal and inland waters: their depth, their habitats and their water quality, and how all of these change over time.

What we do

We primarily develop, test and apply Earth Observation techniques to better map, monitor and understand shallow aquatic systems and support their sustainable management. We work with data collected from satellites, manned aircraft and drones, and do fieldwork to assess the quality of our products. In addition, we do a large amount of other remote sensing research, including on retrogressive thaw slumps, pollinator insects, lava flows, and ticks!

Research themes

All research →

Six themes connect our projects, from method development to operational applications.

Satellite-derived bathymetry

Water depth from optical and lidar satellites, with a focus on the Canadian Arctic.

Habitat mapping

Kelp, seagrass, coral and other benthic habitats from imagery and field data.

Water quality monitoring

Optically active constituents and turbidity tracked through satellite time series.

Change detection

Multi-decadal archives used to quantify change in depth, habitat and water quality.

Self-supervised learning

Representation learning on unlabelled Earth observation data.

Whale detection

Satellite-based detection of North Atlantic right whales for conservation alerts.

  • November 2026

    Since late September, I have been seriously exploring the use of agentic AI in my research. I have done this by creating an “AI lab” in which I decide on the research questions and broad methodological approach for a new research project, and then hand over execution to a “Research Manager” agent who can call on a set of other agents including a “literature agent”, a “coding agent”, a “writing agent”, and several “adversarial reviewer agents” whose job it is to catch mistakes and question assumptions made by their “colleagues”. Most of the lab runs on Claude Code but the reviewer agents include several non-Claude models, a design feature used to ensure that a diversity of models collaborate constructively on the research. Eight weeks in, the lab is concurrently running seven active research projects. The first manuscript is 90% ready and being reviewed by a collaborator, and main results are in for two of the other projects.

    Read the full story

    What have I learned so far? So much, it’s hard to summarize for a news item here but it includes: (1) This kind of lab setup allows me to do research with a scope and speed that would have been completely impossible without it. It feels like someone just gave me an F1 car instead of my old Prius – but for research. (2) The lab is not good at everything: The agents are extremely good at methodological rigour, fantastic at coding, but less good at other things. They should be used for what they excel at, and slowly trained for other tasks as well. (3) Building a functional lab setup takes lots of experimentation. For example: The literature review agent initially only based its understanding of published papers on their abstract. I had to create an explicit rule forcing it to read the full papers, and now it does. Similarly, the writing agent wrote the first draft of a manuscript as if the only audience was itself. I have now created >50 explicit rules it must follow in its writing, and it’s starting to have the writing ability of a mediocre undergraduate student. On the positive side, such rules only need to be put in place once and can be expanded and fine-tuned as the lab matures.

    One thing I haven’t quite figured out yet is how to integrate this lab setup, these agents, into human-to-human collaboration. So far I have just nerded out with this on my own, but it will be much more powerful when it becomes a resource for my entire lab group to experiment with and benefit from. If you have good ideas for how to make that happen please get in touch and let me know!

    — Anders Knudby
